Where it lands

Four costs, one root cause

A descriptor nobody recognizes doesn't stay a merchant problem. It arrives at your bank as contact volume, casework, distorted risk signals, and a worse app experience.

Contact-center volume

“What is this charge?” is among the most common reasons a cardholder calls at all — a fully-loaded contact spent confirming a purchase the customer genuinely made.

Casework that was never fraud

Every unrecognized charge that becomes a claim opens a case, consumes an analyst, and moves through your dispute process at full cost before anyone establishes the transaction was legitimate.

Risk signals you can't trust

Confusion-driven claims enter your systems labeled as fraud. That noise trains models, drives false declines, and buries the genuine fraud your team actually needs to find.

Your app, your reputation

The cryptic string sits in your banking app under your brand. The merchant wrote it, but the cardholder experiences it as your bank failing to tell them where their money went.

Why now

The duty to display sits with issuers

The networks have decided cardholders should see a merchant they recognize — and they have written that obligation onto banks, not merchants. Mastercard's standard is already in force for issuers across the Europe region, requiring the merchant's DBA name, contact details, location, and logo to be shown on request in your app, wallet, or online banking. Visa's counterpart display requirement for issuers follows in January 2027.

  • The four required fields are exactly what Descriptors.com holds
  • The rule creates the duty to display — it does not create the data
  • Issuers can only show what the acquirer and merchant supplied upstream
  • Both rules are European today, but data standards have a long history of traveling
From Mastercard's enhanced merchant data standard

“Friendly fraud is often the result of unrecognizable merchant identifiers in transaction details. Cardholders mistakenly initiate chargebacks because they do not recognize the purchase or merchant descriptor. Many chargebacks may be avoided by providing cardholders with a more detailed description of the transaction.”

Mastercard AN 4569 — Standards for the Display of Enhanced Merchant Data by Issuers

Why the source matters

Declared at the source, not inferred after the fact

Most merchant-identity data reaches a bank by matching a noisy transaction string to the most probable business. The engineering behind that is genuinely good, and it resolves the majority of transactions — but every inference-based approach shares one dependency: it can only be as accurate as the source data underneath it. Where that source is missing, a probable answer is displayed under your brand, and the wrong merchant name is worse for a cardholder than no name at all.

Contributed, not scraped

Records come directly from the acquirers, ISOs, and merchants themselves — parties who know the merchant because they boarded it or operate it. Never scraped from the open web, never inferred by a model.

Timestamped and refreshed monthly

Every record carries its origin and the date it was last confirmed, and the file is refreshed on a monthly cycle. You always know how current the identity you're displaying actually is.

Active participants, not a static file

Contributors aren't a one-time data dump. Acquirers, ISOs, and merchants participate on an ongoing basis, correcting and enriching their own records as businesses change hands, rebrand, or move.

Verified service pathways

The genuinely hard part: confirming the real support phone, chat, email, and refund route for each merchant, one at a time. That work is done upstream so your agents and cardholders can act on it with confidence rather than verifying it themselves.

Beyond a cleaner label

Give a cardholder more than one button

Telling a cardholder who charged them answers the question. It doesn't help the ones who recognize the merchant and still want something done. And today, in almost every banking app, there is exactly one thing they can do about a charge they're unhappy with: dispute it. That single button turns every complaint — however small, however resolvable — into a case on your desk.

  • Request a receipt — resolves the “I don't remember this” case outright
  • Request a refund — routed to the merchant, before a chargeback exists
  • Cancel or pause a subscription — the most common reason recurring charges get disputed
  • Reach merchant support directly — verified phone, chat, and email

Each of these is a resolved concern instead of a case — a materially cheaper outcome than a worked dispute, and a better experience for the cardholder, who gets their answer in minutes rather than waiting out an investigation.

The deflection sequence

Today: unrecognized charge → call your contact center → case opened → chargeback processed → cost absorbed.

With Descriptors.com: unrecognized charge → cardholder sees the verified merchant → resolves it directly → no contact, no case.

Regulation E

Nothing here sits between a cardholder and their right to file. Resolution paths are offered alongside the dispute route — never as a precondition, a gate, or a delay — so your error-resolution obligations and timelines under Regulation E are unchanged. What changes is how many cardholders need to reach for the dispute button at all.

How this is calculated

The ladder is coverage-first. Deflection is applied only to the share of your confusion volume that sits on descriptors the registry already covers — your top-flagged head, set above from your own dispute-volume ranking. Coverage widens as merchants claim descriptors, and the note under the ladder shows the full-coverage figure.

The head is not hypothetical. WhoBilled.me analyzed 192,911 real statement descriptors and built coverage for all 372 descriptors most frequently flagged in card-issuer dispute data — including the aggregator prefixes and processor strings no cardholder can decode. The most-looked-up merchants are household names, which is exactly why the head of your dispute ranking concentrates the way it does. Your dispute-volume ranking and our coverage list are substantially the same list.

Resolution rates in the ladder's range are documented for descriptor-detail programs — Visa reports Order Insight deflecting 40–45% of confirmed first-party-misuse disputes, up to 90% for some subscription merchants. The ladder still treats the rate as a scenario, not a promise.

The $85 default sits inside the commonly cited $75–100+ fully-loaded range for working a dispute. Replace it with your own figure.

Resolution paths sit alongside dispute rights, never in front of them. A descriptor lookup answers the recognition question; nothing about it gates, delays or discourages a cardholder's right to dispute under Reg E, Reg Z or network rules.

This is an arithmetic model of your own inputs, not a performance guarantee or a forecast. Actual results depend on descriptor coverage, your cardholder base and your dispute mix.
